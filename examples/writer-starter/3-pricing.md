## What a wafer-capacity prepayment is

On 2026-08-14, the Open Records Desk reviewed public filings and company statements describing a common arrangement in the semiconductor supply chain: the wafer-capacity prepayment. Under this arrangement, a customer pays a foundry in advance for the right to receive a fixed volume of wafers at a future date. The payment is not a loan and not an equity investment. It is a contractual claim on manufacturing capacity. A prepayment secures future wafer capacity and is carried as a deposit until wafers are delivered. That single sentence, drawn from standard accounting guidance, is the core of the instrument.

The practice is not new. Foundries and their largest customers have used advance payments for decades, particularly when a customer needs assurance that a new process node will be available in volume. The prepayment can cover a portion of the total contract value or the entire amount. It can be refundable or non-refundable, depending on the terms. Public disclosures rarely include the full contract, so the exact terms are often known only to the parties and their auditors.

## How the money is recorded

When a customer makes a prepayment, the foundry does not record the cash as revenue. Instead, the amount is recorded as a deposit, which appears on the foundry's balance sheet as a liability. The customer records the payment as a prepaid asset. As wafers are delivered and accepted, the foundry reduces the deposit liability and recognizes revenue. The customer reduces its prepaid asset and records inventory or cost of goods sold. This matching of cash flow to physical delivery is the accounting logic behind the structure.

The timing of recognition depends on the contract. If the prepayment is tied to specific wafer starts, revenue may be recognized when the wafers are shipped. If the prepayment is tied to capacity reservation alone, revenue may be recognized over the reservation period. Auditors examine whether the deposit is refundable, whether the customer has a right to cancel, and whether the foundry has an obligation to deliver. Those factors determine whether the deposit is classified as current or non-current.

## Why customers and foundries use

For the customer, a prepayment can secure priority access to a constrained process. During periods of high demand, foundries allocate capacity to customers who have committed capital in advance. The prepayment also can lock in a price or a price formula, reducing exposure to spot-market increases. For the foundry, the prepayment provides cash to fund equipment and facility expansion without issuing debt or equity. It also signals demand, which can support internal investment decisions.

The arrangement carries risks for both sides. If the customer's end-market demand falls, the customer may be obligated to take wafers it cannot use, or it may forfeit a portion of the deposit. If the foundry fails to deliver, the customer may have a claim for refund or damages, but recovery can take years. Public filings occasionally disclose disputes over prepayment terms, though most are resolved without litigation.

## Recent examples in public records

In recent years, several foundries have disclosed prepayment arrangements in their annual reports. TSMC, the world's largest contract chipmaker, has reported advance payments from customers in its financial statements, though it does not name them. In 2021 and 2022, during a global chip shortage, multiple automakers and electronics firms announced prepayments to secure capacity. The amounts varied widely. Some were in the hundreds of millions of dollars; others were smaller.

In Japan, the Bank of Japan (BoJ) has published research on corporate prepayment behavior, noting that such arrangements can affect cash-flow statistics. The BoJ's reports do not single out semiconductors, but the pattern is consistent with other capital-intensive industries. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission has brought enforcement actions against companies that misclassified prepayments as revenue, which underscores the importance of the deposit treatment.

On 2026-08-14, no new prepayment announcements were pending in the public record. The most recent disclosure reviewed by the Desk was a quarterly filing from a mid-sized foundry, which reported a deposit balance of approximately $400 million, up from $350 million in the prior quarter. The filing stated that the increase was due to a single customer's prepayment for capacity at a 5-nanometer node. The customer was not named.

## What to watch in future

The Desk will continue to monitor quarterly reports for changes in deposit balances. A sharp increase in deposits may indicate that customers are locking in capacity ahead of a expected demand surge. A sharp decrease may indicate that wafers are being delivered and revenue is being recognized. Neither trend is inherently positive or negative; both are facts to be recorded.

Investors and analysts often ask whether prepayments are a sign of strength or weakness. The public record does not support a single answer. A prepayment can reflect a customer's confidence in future demand, or it can reflect a foundry's need for cash. The only way to know is to read the contract, which is rarely public. Absent that, the deposit line on the balance sheet is the most reliable indicator.

For now, the wafer-capacity prepayment remains a standard tool in the semiconductor supply chain. It is a deposit, not revenue, until wafers are delivered. That is the fact. The Desk will report further developments as they appear in public filings.
