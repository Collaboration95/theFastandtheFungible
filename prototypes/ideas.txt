SINGHACKS 2026 — RIPPLE TRACK PROTOTYPE IDEAS
Updated: 5 September 2026

These are the three active alternatives to the parked DueProof concept.
Each must pass two tests:

1. Remove the agent: does the experience collapse into an ordinary app?
2. Replace XRPL/x402 with a card or API key: does the autonomous workflow or

==========================================================================
IDEA 1 — FAIRCUT
==========================================================================

Pitch
-----
An AI editor licenses the exact seconds it uses—and the creator gets paid
immediately.

Person
------
Leah is a Singapore-based independent filmmaker finishing a 20-second travel
campaign at midnight. She needs a 12-second music cue that can be used
commercially in Singapore and Japan. She cannot spend an hour interpreting
catalogue licences, and she does not want her editor to use an unlicensed or
provenance-unknown track.

Problem
-------
AI can assemble media faster than people can clear the underlying rights.
Existing licensing is usually organized around large catalogues,
subscriptions, and human checkout flows. Independent creators also face
transaction costs that make tiny, narrowly scoped licences uneconomic.

Agent mandate
-------------
"Find a tense but hopeful 12-second cue for this cut. Commercial social use in
Singapore and Japan for six months. Budget S$1. No voice replicas and no asset
without verifiable provenance."

What the agent does
-------------------
1. Understands the edit's timing, mood, and sonic gaps.
2. Discovers independent licensed stems from multiple providers.
3. Auditions watermarked previews against the actual cut.
4. Parses permissions, prohibitions, attribution duties, territory, term,
   provenance, and price.
5. Rejects creatively strong tracks that do not satisfy the rights mandate.
6. Purchases the best eligible 12-second licence.
7. Inserts the clean stem and returns the finished cut plus a rights receipt.

What is purchased
-----------------
One x402-protected package containing:

- the clean 12-second audio stem;
- an ISCC content identifier or cryptographic asset hash;
- a machine-readable ODRL licence;
- a C2PA Content Credentials provenance manifest, where available;
- creator/payee identity and required attribution text;
- licence and delivery hashes.

Why Ripple, t54, and ClawCredit belong
--------------------------------------
- XRPL enables a genuinely small, cross-border, machine-native purchase and
  immediate creator settlement. The payment binds the licence order; private
  media and full licence details remain off-ledger.
- t54/Trustline checks the mandate, asset identifier, rights-holder payee,
  price, territory, term, and delivered licence. Changed rights invalidate the
  original approval.
- t54's Agentic Risk Standard supplies the fee lifecycle: authorize payment,
  deliver the asset and licence, evaluate their hashes and rights, then mark
  the job fulfilled or exceptional.
- ClawCredit is an optional path for an eligible editor agent without a
  pre-funded wallet. Do not make it critical unless live onboarding, payment,
  and repayment are proven.

Industry standards
------------------
- W3C ODRL: permissions, prohibitions, constraints, payment and attribution
  duties — https://www.w3.org/TR/odrl-model/
- ISO 24138:2024 ISCC: identification of digital media assets —
  https://committee.iso.org/standard/77899.html
- C2PA Content Credentials: signed media provenance and edit history —
  https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/

Blocked path
------------
The most attractive S$0.20 track permits personal use only and its C2PA signer
does not match the claimed rights-holder:

BLOCKED
Creative match: strong
Commercial rights: absent
Rights-holder/payee binding: failed
No payment was signed or submitted.

The agent selects a S$0.60 eligible cue, pays on XRP Testnet, unlocks it, and
the audience hears the finished cut.

Three-minute demo
-----------------
1. Play the silent or rough 20-second edit.
2. Show three watermarked cues evaluated inside the cut.
3. Block the most attractive cue for a rights mismatch.
4. Show the eligible licence and Leah's bounded mandate.
5. Pay on XRP Testnet through one pinned x402 flow.
6. Replace the watermark with the clean stem and play the final cut.
7. Open the receipt: user intent -> ODRL rights -> XRPL hash -> asset hash ->
   creator paid.

Irreducibility test
-------------------
- Without the agent, FairCut becomes manual stock-music search and checkout.
  The creative decision inside the actual edit disappears.
- A centralized catalogue could use cards. The MVP must therefore show
  independent providers and a truly small per-use purchase with immediate
  creator settlement, not one fake marketplace balance.

Primary risks
-------------
- C2PA proves signed provenance assertions, not copyright ownership.
- Use original media owned by a consenting creator and a deliberately narrow
  demo licence; do not make unsupported legal claims.
- A single self-hosted catalogue weakens the XRPL argument.

Strategic score: 43/45
Best quality: most elegant and strongest software-only stage transformation.


==========================================================================
IDEA 2 — FLEETCREDIT
==========================================================================

### Core pitch

**A fleet vehicle with no payment history can earn just enough credit to buy the energy it needs to finish its job.**

The interesting part is **not EV charging**.

The interesting part is:

> How do autonomous machines participate economically when you don't want to preload every machine with money or give every machine an unrestricted corporate card?

That gives FleetCredit a substantially bigger thesis.

A fleet might provision hundreds or thousands of:

* delivery vehicles,
* rental cars,
* service vans,
* autonomous vehicles,
* eventually robots/drones/machines.

A newly provisioned vehicle knows its mission and belongs to a verified fleet, but it may have:

**no charger accounts, no payment history, no prepaid wallet and no relationship with the charging provider it encounters.**

Yet during a job it may genuinely need to spend money.

The fleet operator shouldn't have to give every vehicle an unlimited wallet.

That's the problem.

---

## The user

Suppose:

**SwiftMed** operates a fleet delivering time-sensitive medical supplies.

Vehicle **V-204** has just joined the fleet.

It has never made a payment before.

Its current mission is:

> Deliver a temperature-sensitive package to NUS Hospital by 7:15 PM.

During the route:

**battery = 8%**

Based on current traffic and consumption:

> Vehicle cannot complete the job with required reserve.

Now the agent needs to act.

---

# The agent isn't a charger finder

This distinction is critical.

Google Maps can find chargers.

FleetCredit's agent has to answer:

> **What economic action gives me the highest probability of completing my mission within the authority my operator gave me?**

It therefore reasons over:

| Factor                  | Example                 |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Remaining range         | 21 km                   |
| Destination distance    | 28 km                   |
| Required safety reserve | 10 km                   |
| Delivery deadline       | 7:15 PM                 |
| Charger detour          | 2–8 minutes             |
| Predicted queue         | 0–20 minutes            |
| Charging speed          | 50–150 kW               |
| Connector               | CCS2                    |
| Energy required         | 7–10 kWh                |
| Charging price          | S$4–S$11                |
| Occupancy penalties     | varies                  |
| Merchant/operator trust | verified / unknown      |
| Available balance       | **S$0**                 |
| Permitted borrowing     | bounded by fleet policy |

Now this becomes an agent problem.

---

# The mission mandate

The fleet owner gives the agent standing authority along the lines of:

> Complete assigned deliveries while maintaining at least 10 km reserve. You may spend or borrow up to S$12 for energy when necessary to meet a confirmed mission. Only use approved charging operators. Never accept occupancy fees above S$0.20/min.

That's nice because everything is constrained:

**purpose + amount + merchant + circumstance + expiry.**

And that's very aligned with the sponsor material's emphasis on bounded authority rather than giving an agent unrestricted wallet access. 

---

This is where your demo becomes much stronger than the original LastPercent.

2-minute detour
S$4.20
Cheap

But:

**17-minute predicted queue**

Estimated arrival:

**7:24 PM — mission fails.**

---

### Charger B

**VoltFast**

5-minute detour
S$7.40
No queue
150 kW

Estimated arrival:

**7:09 PM**

Mission succeeds.

---

### Charger C

3-minute detour
S$5.90

But when final payment terms arrive:

> Occupancy fee: S$0.45/min
> Payment destination differs from registered operator

The agent blocks it.

This gives you the safe negative path the hackathon explicitly likes: changed terms or destination cannot silently pass simply because the LLM thinks the charger is attractive. 

---

# Then comes the actual differentiating moment

The agent selects B.

Price:

**S$7.40**

Wallet:

**S$0.00**

And now the interface says:

> **Insufficient operating balance. Requesting mission credit.**

This is where FleetCredit begins.

---

# Cold-start machine credit

The vehicle has **no repayment history**.

But zero financial history does not mean zero information.

The underwriting decision can conceptually consider:

**Who owns the vehicle?**
SwiftMed — verified fleet.

**Is there a real task?**
Yes — active dispatch #MED-4182.

**Is the expense relevant?**
Yes — projected range insufficient.

**Is the amount proportional?**
S$7.40 against S$12 mandate.

**Is the counterparty legitimate?**
Approved charging operator.

**What happens without the purchase?**
Predicted missed delivery.

**What exactly is being financed?**
A specific charging authorization.

So you can display something like:

> **INITIAL CREDIT APPROVED — S$8.00**
> Reason: verified fleet + active mission + bounded energy purchase
> Maximum exposure: S$8
> Valid for: 20 minutes
> Merchant: VoltFast #SG-1042

That is much more compelling than:

> “AI got a crypto loan.”

It becomes:

> **A machine with no financial history can still be creditworthy because there is meaningful context around what it is, who owns it and what it is trying to accomplish.**

That is very close to the interesting part of the t54 research. Their ClawCredit framing similarly evaluates more than historical payments—runtime/security/reasoning signals are involved, new agents start conservatively, and capacity can grow with successful repayment behaviour.  

---

# The long-term FleetCredit idea

This is where the concept gets interesting beyond the hackathon.

Vehicle V-204 initially receives:

**Credit capacity: S$10**

Then it completes:

> charge → mission → repayment

multiple times.

Its machine economic history grows.

Eventually:

**S$10 → S$25 → S$50**

Meanwhile another newly provisioned vehicle begins again with conservative capacity.

So you start getting:

### Machine creditworthiness.

Not:

> “What is this wallet's balance?”

but:

> **“How much economic authority should this machine have right now?”**

That's a genuinely interesting product thesis.

Ripple's lending research itself separates **credit judgment off-chain** from standardized execution/records, which fits this conceptual split very cleanly. 

---

# FleetCredit demo features

Because you're time-constrained, I would only expose about **five things** in the UI.

| Demo surface                       | What judges understand                |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Mission + battery                  | Why something needs to happen         |
| Charger comparison                 | Agent actually makes a decision       |
| Mission-credit request             | Why credit exists                     |
| Approved / blocked economic action | t54-style bounded autonomy            |
| Route/mission outcome              | Something real happened after payment |

Everything else can be backend/state.

You absolutely do **not** need:

fleet management dashboards, historical analytics, user management, billing screens, charger administration, maps with dozens of stations, loan management interfaces, etc.

# FleetCredit's weakness

This is important when discussing it with the professor.

The current ClawCredit material explicitly focuses on x402 resources such as **compute, data and premium APIs**, rather than general-purpose P2P payments. 

So conceptually FleetCredit is extremely aligned with **t54's research problem**, but less directly aligned with **ClawCredit's currently documented product surface**.

I would describe it to the professor as:

> **“We're applying t54's cold-start agent underwriting concept to autonomous machines purchasing physical services.”**

and ask whether they consider that a desirable extension.

==========================================================================
IDEA 3 — RESEARCHAGENT
==========================================================================

I think your new description makes this considerably stronger.

And interestingly, **ResearchAgent fits t54's current ClawCredit/x402 world more directly than FleetCredit**, because premium data and paid APIs are explicitly among the kinds of services that ClawCredit is designed around. 

## Core pitch

### **Give an AI a research question and a budget. It decides which evidence is worth paying for.**

Or:

> **ResearchAgent turns a research budget into the strongest evidence dossier it can buy.**

This is much better than:

> “AI that bypasses paywalls.”

Absolutely avoid that framing.

The whole product philosophy is actually the opposite:

### **Good information should get paid for.**

Publishers spend significant money producing:

* investigative journalism;
* financial reporting;
* expert interviews;
* industry analysis;
* specialist databases;
* academic research.

Meanwhile AI research tools create an awkward situation:

> Some of the best evidence is behind a paywall.

So models either:

* don't see it;
* rely on weaker secondary reporting;
* repeatedly cite the same accessible sources;
* or users personally maintain many subscriptions.

Your proposition is:

> **Let expert research agents purchase individual pieces of high-value information when those sources materially improve the answer.**

That is a real new economic model.

---

# Who it's for

Don't position this as:

> “Perplexity but paid articles.”

Go much narrower and more serious.

### ResearchAgent is for professional knowledge work.

Examples:

**fund managers**

> “Assess whether Company X's margin expansion is sustainable through FY2027.”

**professors**

> “Build an evidence dossier on the economic effects of semiconductor export controls.”

**consultants**

> “Determine whether the Indonesian data-centre market can support another hyperscale entrant.”

**corporate strategy teams**

> “Assess whether competitor X is genuinely entering our market.”

These users care about **source quality** much more than ordinary web-search users.

And their research has economic value.

So spending:

**S$2, S$10 or S$25**

to materially improve a dossier is not absurd.

---

# ResearchAgent's core intelligence

The agent has two scarce resources:

### attention

and

### money.

The clever part is deciding:

> **What information should I buy next?**

Suppose the user gives:

> **Research question:**
> Is NVIDIA's current data-centre growth sustainable through 2027?

> **Research budget:**
> S$5.

The agent starts from open information.

Maybe it discovers 30 sources.

For each candidate it has signals like:

| Signal                    | Meaning                                          |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Semantic relevance        | Does this actually answer the research question? |
| Authority                 | FT vs random blog                                |
| Recency                   | Is this current enough?                          |
| Originality               | Primary reporting or rewritten material?         |
| Expected information gain | Is it likely to add something we don't know?     |
| Corroboration value       | Can it independently confirm another claim?      |
| Contradiction value       | Could it challenge the emerging thesis?          |
| Redundancy                | Do we already have equivalent evidence?          |
| Price                     | What does unlocking it cost?                     |
| Remaining budget          | What opportunities are we giving up?             |

This is where the agent is genuinely doing **economic research planning**.

---

# The key mechanic: expected evidence value

Imagine the system finds these:

| Source                         | Price |     Trust | Relevance |       Novelty |
| ------------------------------ | ----: | --------: | --------: | ------------: |
| NVIDIA earnings release        |  Free | Very high |      High |        Medium |
| Reuters report                 | $0.20 |      High |      High |        Medium |
| Tech blog                      |  Free |    Medium |      High |           Low |
| FT semiconductor investigation | $0.90 | Very high | Very high |      **High** |
| Specialist supply-chain report | $2.50 | Very high |    Medium | **Very high** |
| Random newsletter              | $0.50 |       Low |    Medium |           Low |

The important behavior is that ResearchAgent does **not** simply buy everything.

Maybe:

> Reuters adds useful corroboration for $0.20 → BUY.

> FT contains exclusive supplier commentary directly relevant to the thesis for $0.90 → BUY.

> Newsletter largely repeats known reporting → SKIP.

> $2.50 supply-chain report looks excellent, but would consume half the remaining budget → defer until we identify whether this is actually the missing evidence.

This is agentic.

---

# Baseline versus premium pricing

Your baseline-price / good-article-price concept works well.

I'd make it a very simple publisher market.

### Standard article

For normal useful journalism:

**S$0.05–S$0.25**

### Premium evidence

For:

* investigative pieces;
* exclusive interviews;
* specialist financial reporting;
* expert analysis;
* primary research;

maybe:

**S$0.50–S$2**

The exact numbers don't matter in the demo.

What's important is that **publishers choose the price**, while the researcher chooses a budget.

Then the agent decides whether the marginal evidence is worth paying for.

That's a far more interesting marketplace than:

> “everything costs 10 cents.”

# The really strong ResearchAgent feature: Budget Modes

For the demo, I think this could be fantastic.

Same question.

Three budgets.

### S$0 — Open Web

Agent produces:

**Confidence: moderate**

Sources:

SEC filings
company release
open blogs
free reporting

One conclusion remains unresolved.

---

### S$1 — Analyst

Agent spends:

$0.20 Reuters
$0.70 FT

> supplier commentary independently confirms demand but reveals a capacity constraint.

Conclusion becomes more nuanced.

---

### S$5 — Deep Research

Agent additionally purchases a specialist dataset/report.

It now discovers evidence contradicting the initial thesis.

Final conclusion changes.

That's powerful.

You're literally showing:

> **better research has a cost, and the agent decides where that money generates the most information value.**

---

# And this solves an important AI problem

Today a research agent can easily return:

**15 sources**

that are actually:

**one original report + 14 articles quoting it.**

ResearchAgent should care about **evidence independence**.

For example:

> Bloomberg reports supplier shortage.

Then five blogs quote Bloomberg.

The agent should understand:

> This is **one evidentiary source**, not six confirmations.

Now suppose FT independently interviewed another supplier.

That article may be worth buying because it provides **independent corroboration**.

This is exactly the sort of behavior professional users would value.

---

# The dossier

The output shouldn't be a chat answer.

Call it a:

# **Research Dossier**

Top-level conclusion.

Then something like:

**Thesis**

> Data-centre growth remains strong, but evidence indicates power and packaging constraints create material downside risk beyond 2027.

**Confidence**

High.

**Evidence**

8 independent sources.

**Paid evidence**

3 sources — S$1.45 total.

**Contradictory evidence**

2 sources.

**Unresolved questions**

1.

And then claims are linked to their underlying sources.

For a fund manager/professor, that's substantially more useful than a blob of generated prose.

---

# Give the user control over research policy

One elegant feature is a **Research Mandate**.

For example:

> Research NVIDIA data-centre sustainability.
> Budget: **S$5**
> Prefer: filings, Reuters, FT, Bloomberg, academic sources
> Avoid: anonymous blogs
> Pay up to S$1 automatically per source
> Ask me before any source above S$1
> Stop once additional evidence is unlikely to materially change the conclusion.

That last sentence is excellent:

> **Stop when marginal information value falls below marginal research cost.**

That's basically the whole product.

---

You need only:

# Why XRPL actually fits ResearchAgent

This has perhaps the cleanest XRPL argument after FairCut.

Right now publishers typically sell:

> $30/month publication subscription.

But an autonomous research agent may want:

> exactly one article from FT
> two articles from Reuters
> one industry report
> one dataset query

It shouldn't have to maintain:

**27 subscriptions + accounts + credentials + renewal states.**

Instead publishers expose machine-readable research resources.

Agent asks:

> article?

Publisher responds:

> S$0.20.

Agent decides whether the article's expected research value justifies S$0.20.

Pays.

Reads.

Moves on.

That is precisely the kind of **machine-native service purchase** that x402 makes conceptually clean, and premium data is already within the service categories contemplated in the t54 material. 

And XRPL gives you the common machine settlement rail rather than maintaining a bilateral account with every publisher.

### Version A — user-funded research budget

> S$5.

This is the **simpler and stronger MVP story**.

### Version B — ResearchAgent earns a research line

The enterprise says:

> This agent is permitted to conduct research but we don't pre-fund every agent.

It gets:

**S$2 initial research credit.**

It produces useful dossiers.

Over time:

> $2 → $10 → $25 autonomous research capacity.

That incorporates the t54 thesis around credit capacity growing with trusted behaviour and repayment.

The killer ResearchAgent concept is:

> **optimal allocation of a research budget.**

Not:

> lending money to an AI.
