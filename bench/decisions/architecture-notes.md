## Current privacy and spending boundaries

The ordinary benchmark candidate state in `run.ts:12–17` explicitly assembles question, gap, sanitized read-source references and `clefCandidate(publicCandidate(...))`. Batch state at lines 33–36 uses the same projection for each candidate. The OpenAI adapter's candidate method at `providers/openai-decisions.ts:39–41` applies that projection again. The actual model-visible candidate fields are resource ID, title, publisher, abstract, tags, tier, family, optional derivedFrom/relevance and authority. Price, wallet, URL, manifest, body, spans, reputation, gold labels and paidLabel are excluded. Public-source references contain ID, version, title, publisher, family and facets, never source body.

An offline sentinel check passed a candidate containing a hidden body, spans, labels, wallet and URL through production parsing/projection. The projected payload contained no hidden sentinel and only the allowed public fields. This is a payload-boundary check, not a general proof against text-level leakage. Candidate abstracts remain untrusted text, including deliberately planted price anchors and instructions. Excluding the structured price field does not remove a quoted price from adversarial abstract text; the benchmark should distinguish structural price-blindness from resistance to price persuasion.

The previously documented **119 public-preview/body eight-word overlaps belong to the synthetic benchmark construction**. No production corpus or leak threshold was changed. This experiment may establish that a body field was not sent to a candidate endpoint; it cannot claim production `sharesRun` abstract-leak compliance for those synthetic rows. Body-positive and public-promise-positive labels are intentionally separate on overclaim cases.

Benchmark paid tasks send `{question,gap,passages:[body]}` only to the separate paid-relevance call (`run.ts:47`); these are labelled synthetic granted-body fixtures, not actual purchases or grants. The independent DeepSeek QA source constructs a combined public-plus-delivered-body audit context (`qa.ts:10–11`). It is a separate label audit, not a public-only candidate arm; instructions to judge its public label from the abstract do not make that audit context technically body-blind. Do not describe QA as enforcing the same public-only payload boundary as candidate evaluation.

Production enforces a stronger runtime boundary. `loop.ts:170` rejects non-free content returned by retrieval; `accessible()` at lines 81–86 requires free identity or matching run/resource/version grant plus VERIFIED intent. Purchases store delivery then check its proof (`purchases.ts:208–212`); existence of a stored delivery alone does not make it model-accessible. `Reputation.calibrate()` at `reputation.ts:82–95` independently requires VERIFIED intent, matching grant, content and a paid candidate before sending cloned paid content. Any future cache or parallel task must preserve these checks on a cache hit as well as a miss.

The benchmark invokes actual `decide()` offline; it never invokes `Purchases`, reserves a payment or signs a transaction. Model outputs supply judgments only. Production policy multiplies trust, filters rewrite/acquired/no-gap/cap/value/budget conditions and chooses one resource by value per SGD. Transactional purchase reservation and the Stop checks remain the authority for spending, including retries. Cached judgments must never become cached purchase authorizations.

The experimental OpenAI provider uses the legacy `name:'cloudflare'` discriminator to satisfy the current interface, with `model:'gpt-6-luna'`; artifacts identify `arm:'luna'`. This is explicitly research-only. Wiring it directly into production would mislabel the UI's provider name until the discriminator/display mapping is extended. It is not implemented as a production integration.

Transport allows decision HTTP requests up to 20 seconds and flags `latencyMs > 3000` after completion (`transport.ts:72,80`). The adapter/runner reject those late decision outputs for policy; this provides observed uncensored latency and retrospective three-second availability classification. It does **not** execute a three-second network abort. Production Clef has a three-second limit **per attempt**, up to two attempts, and 429 backoff of up to sixty seconds (`clef.ts:66–90`); account discovery can add another request. Neither path establishes a three-second whole-round maximum. Do not call the benchmark a faithful measurement of production retry/cancellation latency.

Unbatched requests launch with `Promise.all`, but the experimental limiter admits only two requests per provider and spaces starts by approximately 410 ms. Clef and Clef-flash share the same Cloudflare bucket, including when their arms run concurrently; DeepSeek QA and OpenAI use separate buckets. `decisionElapsedMs` therefore includes self-imposed admission delay, contention between experimental arms, account setup and cached lookup. It stops before paid tasks and policy selection. `networkLowerBoundMs` is the maximum individual decision-request duration after admission, not server compute time and not real end-to-end production latency. Batch wall-time gains can largely reflect fewer limiter admissions; report request/token/cost changes separately from round wall time.

`run.ts` obtains two paid audit observations per scenario regardless of the selected resource, including no-purchase rounds. These are calibration evaluation examples; production generally scores one verified purchase's passages. The live branch records decision elapsed before these audits but does not run `select()` until they finish. Do not interpret total `evaluate()` duration as production purchase-decision latency or multiply two synthetic paid audits per scenario into a claim about ordinary production cost. The analyzer source separates decision-call and paid-audit cost and treats late/error paid outputs as missing; raw runner paid probability alone is insufficient to classify a timely successful paid judgment.

Failure handling uses whole-round fixture substitution via actual `decide()`, rather than combining successful provider candidates with fixture candidates. Fallback remains visible in saved policy metadata and production `fallbackReason`. One nuance: experimental `select()` always passes an explicit arm threshold, so fixture substitution inherits flash 0.15 / clef 0.35 / luna 0.20. Production with no explicit threshold/environment override recomputes its fallback threshold from `metadata-fixture`, yielding 0.20. This is an explicit-threshold replay of production policy, not necessarily the default production fallback configuration. Report it when interpreting failure-inclusive comparisons; inspect deployment configuration separately before claiming exact production parity.

## Proposed production changes: bounded opportunities

### 1. Prefilter only a deterministically empty gap

Current `decideRound()` calls the provider before checking `!gap.trim()` (`decision.ts:93–119`). A mock-only check with one candidate counted two provider methods even for an empty gap, then returned no selection and `SKIP_NO_GAP`. With six candidates, a prefilter could avoid seven remote decision requests for that particular round. This is a source-derived request-count bound, not a measured latency/USD saving. The frozen corpus has only two dedicated no-gap cases; their frequency does not estimate production incidence.

Use `!gap.trim()` as a deterministic trigger. Do not equate zero budget, apparent answer completeness or a keyword heuristic with no material gap. For nonempty gaps, a sequential round-first materiality test could avoid six candidate calls if its result is exactly zero, but it adds round-call latency to every positive round. A positive soft cutoff changes policy, can miss evidence and would need a separate dev-only evaluation; it is not an authorized gate relaxation.

Preserve the verdict table and reason precedence: quarantined sellers and known rewrites remain SKIP_LOW_TRUST/SKIP_REWRITE before SKIP_NO_GAP, including UC1's rewrite display. A proposed deterministic prefilter should emit honestly labelled unscored candidate judgment placeholders and metadata-based known rewrites rather than claim remote originality/credibility inference happened. Stop spending remains a ledger concern. With a material gap and zero budget, retain candidate judgments: the production UI intentionally shows `SKIP_OVER_BUDGET` and `wouldBuy:true` for a source that otherwise clears value/cap/trust. The mock confirmed null selection with that wouldBuy row. `Answer.tsx:109–116`, `RunTape.tsx:46–50` and `DecisionTable.tsx:43–44` consume this behavior.

### 2. Batch the round and candidate judgments

The implemented experimental batch packs one materiality predicate plus three questions for each of six candidates: seven decision requests become one, a theoretical 85.7% request-count reduction. With two independent paid audits, total experimental calls change from nine to three only when those audit calls are also enabled. Input tokens, cost and latency savings do not follow proportionally: a larger state, indexed instructions and all candidate abstracts remain billable.

This is also a semantic change. Every candidate can see the conclusion and the other candidates, unlike the isolated public candidate calls. Cross-candidate persuasion, order effects and correlated failures become possible. An index-specific instruction does not enforce context isolation. Require exact question/resource-ID mapping, full validation, a tested chunk boundary and visible fallback on a malformed response. Retain public-only projection and keep granted passages in a separate request. Do not place price/wallet/budget/cap in the shared batch state to help ranking; deterministic code still applies trust, price and spending constraints afterward.

Production adoption would require a versioned provider capability such as `judgeBatch`, while preserving separate-call providers and current fallback labeling. Candidate order and option-order effects need independent tests; the current originality-option diagnostic is not a test of candidate-list position bias in a shared batch. Completed full-dev observations are summarized below; production adoption still requires held-out evidence that the quota stop prevented for Cloudflare.

### 3. Cache successful candidate judgments by exact public context

Production currently caches account discovery, not candidate judgments. The experimental full-request cache already reuses identical public states, but it is an archival observation cache keyed additionally by repeat/ordinal, not a designed production TTL/invalidation policy.

A production key must include provider/model/deployment identity, question-wording/schema version, exact question and gap, a signature of **all model-visible read-source fields** (ID, version, title, publisher, family, facets), and the exact projected candidate fields plus resource version as an invalidation guard. Gap plus resource ID alone is insufficient. Originality changes when newly acquired sources enter the read list; public previews, family, derivedFrom, authority and relevance can change too. Canonicalizing source/tag order is safe only if the actual model request uses that same canonical order; otherwise order-sensitive outputs must not be conflated.

Cache raw validated judgments, then apply current calibration, threshold, wallet trust, quarantine status, acquired IDs, price/cap/budget and Stop state each time. Do not cache final rows, winner, wouldBuy or purchase intent as authorization. A price-only change need not invalidate price-blind inference but must recompute policy ranking. Trust changes likewise require policy recomputation even when inference is reused. Use TTL/version invalidation and in-flight request sharing; evict rejected promises so transient failures do not poison the key. A fixture substitute must not be saved under a successful live-model cache key.

This proposal can reduce repeated inference but has no measured production hit rate or saving in the current benchmark. Multi-round read-source changes may make hit rates much lower than candidate-ID overlap suggests. Any separate paid-body cache must check the current run's verified grant before returning a hit and must be kept out of public-candidate cache namespaces.

### 4. Clear failed promises without hiding fallback history

For the actual account-discovery bugs above, retain the current successful shared promise; on rejection, clear it only if the stored promise is still that failed instance, then rethrow. Retry/backoff should stay bounded, avoiding a request storm. The experimental transport also needs unconditional slot cleanup around discovery and file persistence; writing a cache/meter must not become another route that strands limiter capacity.

Preserve failed raw responses and their timestamps for scientific reliability accounting. Clearing an in-memory failed promise is different from deleting forensic HTTP failures and silently repeating a benchmark observation. Retries need their own recorded attempt/ordinal and cost. Do not cache a whole-round fixture fallback as a live success, and keep fallback labels visible even when later requests recover. A per-provider circuit breaker may reduce repeated unavailable-provider cost, but must identify substituted decisions and preserve the fixture/policy/zero-budget behavior rather than synthesize live-looking confidence.

### 5. Overlap postpurchase calibration with writing the validated answer

Current `loop.ts:212–216` awaits paid-body calibration before calling `answer()`. A failure skips the trust update and is labelled in `Reputation.calibrate()`, so it does not fail the answer, but its successful/timeout wall time still delays the answer. Neither source comment saying “never blocks the run” nor the current benchmark decision-only elapsed metric means zero latency impact.

After proof verification, proof trust update and grant checks, start calibration and answer generation concurrently. Await calibration before the **next purchase decision** so the current round still observes the updated trust multiplier; await or persist pending calibration before final run completion. The potential overlap is at most the shorter of calibration time and answer-generation time; no milliseconds of gain were measured here. Keep proof/challenge/quarantine operations synchronous in the authorization path. Answer generation uses independently verified content and does not need the soft relevance score to establish citation access.

An unrestricted fire-and-forget calibration job would change subsequent purchase behavior and lose updates on process exit. The current calibration method has no explicit once-per-intent update guard; `recordCalibration()` increments its count each invocation. Any asynchronous/restart-capable implementation needs an atomic persisted intent/resource/version/provider-context job identity, idempotent application, retained original gap/public relevance promise, cancellation/lifecycle policy and a labelled pending/skipped status. Stop must halt purchases immediately; an already-granted content score need not authorize any new spend. No premium text may be fetched merely to feed this background task.

## Priority and evidence needed

Among future production optimizations, a narrowly empty-gap prefilter and failed-promise eviction are the smallest changes with clear source evidence. Candidate-context caching requires a measured hit-rate experiment; batch behavior has implemented experimental configurations but changes context; calibration/answer overlap requires preserved trust ordering and durable once-only updates. None of these optimizations was applied by this worker, and none of their production latency/cost gains has been measured here.

Every proposal keeps public-only pre-purchase inference, deterministic spending authorization, one charge per intent, exact granted-span citations and explicit substitution labels. The benchmark cannot test payment/proof/citation execution merely by calling offline `decide()`; the production regression slice remains selection-only as documented in `data-notes.md`.
