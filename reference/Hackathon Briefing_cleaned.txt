[Oliver Wyman — Actuarial / Communications Partner video, Speaker 5, 19:07-25:14 — cleaned]

Agentic AI is moving from experimental to operating [in insurance]. Becoming straightforward to build. Underwriting: agent takes submission, extracts exposures, compares against appetite, pulls third-party data, drafts broker questions, proposes terms. What it cannot do is bind. Submission moves at machine speed. Authority to commit does not. Claims FNOL: agent collects documents, checks coverage, flags inconsistencies, recommends reserve, drafts communication, assembles settlement package. Work runs up to where someone must decide and stops. More than automation — changes work. People stop performing every step, start supervising agents: setting authority, reviewing exceptions, challenging recommendations, deciding what may bind. Deep underwriting/claims judgment stays scarce. Added skill: knowing when machine is wrong.

Industry asked whether AI can do this work. Agents are good enough — question moved: can we let it do this work at scale on decisions carrying real money?

Precedent: Volvo didn't invent three-point seatbelt to make cars stand still — to make speed safer. Good AI governance same — not wrap agents in process nobody uses, but build confidence to move faster. Risk changes shape when AI moves inside decision, not beside it — selects what decision-maker sees, weights options, frames recommendation. Human approval is necessary but not evidence of independent evaluation.

Ask: 18 months from now, could you reconstruct that decision? Show which model/version acted, what data it saw, what authority it held, what evidence it relied, where human overrode? If not, exposure is hard to price, reserve, supervise, transfer.

Consequential AI needs capability, architecture-agnostic, specific about evidence. Five things:
1. Attributability — every consequential decision leaves durable, independently verifiable record of what was decided, by which authorized actor, on what basis.
2. Detectability — drift, degradation, silent substitution surfaced by design, not presumed absent.
3. Bounded severity — failure contained in finite envelope.
4. No single trust group — model may be capable, even dominant, without becoming what we simply believe. Models inform, authorized actors bind.
5. Demonstrability — decision can be rebuilt end-to-end: model, version, inputs, authority, evidence.

Delivery: deliberately model-agnostic thin control layer above models, every consequential action passing one point answering: Who may bind? What is recorded? How change becomes visible? How assurance scales with consequence? When authority absent/expired, nothing binds. Every action leaves signed, hash-chained, reconstructable receipt. Same control point sees workload/task class/model/cost — produces second ledger. AI spend is board question, mostly answered with after-the-fact allocation. One control point, two ledgers.

Architecture not enough. Challenge capability must be built/staffed, not assumed. Controls must be easier to use than bypass. Review capacity must not be engineered out by business case. Instrumented firm with nobody to run it documents failure without preventing it.

Matters in insurance because AI exposure doesn't stay local — same models, clouds, pipelines, prompt patterns. Few common dependencies produce correlated failure across books. Cyber taught: concentrated capability creates concentrated exposure. Capability may be concentrated. Trust need not be.

Path to insurability: make AI users visible, failure detectable/challengeable, controls enforceable, bound exposure so it can be assured, in time partly transferred. Only subset will be; rest must be governed.

Winners will not be firms with most agents, but firms whose agents operate with speed, evidence, authority, accountability. Next frontier is not more capable AI, but AI institutions can trust enough to use at scale.

— Trust and AI at scale, Oliver Wyman Actuarial.
